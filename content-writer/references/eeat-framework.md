# E-E-A-T Framework — General Reference

## What Is E-E-A-T?

E-E-A-T stands for **Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness**. It is Google's quality framework used by human quality raters (via the Search Quality Rater Guidelines, QRG) and increasingly by the core ranking system itself. It is not a direct ranking factor but a rubric for evaluating content quality. Strong E-E-A-T signals correlate with better visibility, especially for YMYL (Your Money or Your Life) topics like health, finance, safety.

---

## The Four Factors

### 1. Experience (First-hand Knowledge)

Does the content show that the creator has direct, lived experience with the topic?

| Strong Signals | Weak Signals |
|---|---|
| Original research, case studies, before/after documentation | Generic rephrasing of common knowledge |
| Personal anecdotes with specific, verifiable details | Vague claims with no concrete examples |
| Process documentation (e.g., step-by-step with real context) | No indication the author has ever done what they describe |
| Unique data, proprietary insights (e.g., survey results, user testing) | Statistics copied from other sources without attribution |
| Photos, videos, or recordings from direct experience | Stock photos only, no original media |

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Does the author demonstrate they've *done* what they're writing about?
- [ ] Are there specific, concrete examples that could not come from a general knowledge source?
- [ ] If it's a how-to or review, is there evidence of actual use?

### 2. Expertise (Knowledge & Qualifications)

Does the content demonstrate appropriate depth of knowledge?

| Strong Signals | Weak Signals |
|---|---|
| Author credentials, certifications, professional licenses | No author byline or generic team name |
| Professional background relevant to the topic | Author bio with unrelated credentials |
| Technical depth appropriate for the target audience | Too shallow (surface-level) or too academic for the reader |
| Accurate, well-sourced claims with proper terminology | Terminology used incorrectly or inconsistently |
| Depth that a knowledgeable professional would recognize as correct | Factual errors or oversimplifications |

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Is the author qualified to write on this topic? (Credentials visible?)
- [ ] Is the technical level appropriate for the intended audience?
- [ ] Are all factual claims accurate and properly sourced?

### 3. Authoritativeness (Reputation & Recognition)

Is the content creator / website recognized as a go-to source?

| Strong Signals | Weak Signals |
|---|---|
| External citations, backlinks from authoritative sites | No external sources or only low-quality links |
| Brand mentions, industry recognition | Unknown brand with no external presence |
| Published in or referenced by recognized outlets | Only self-references |
| Cited by other experts in the field | No evidence of being cited or referenced |
| Official credentials verifiable via external profiles (LinkedIn, PubMed, etc.) | Credentials claimed but unverifiable |

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Do other authoritative sites link to or cite this content?
- [ ] Is the author/brand verifiable outside the site itself?
- - [ ] Are sources authoritative for the topic (e.g., PubMed for health, government sites for legal)?

### 4. Trustworthiness (Transparency & Accuracy)

Can the user trust the content and the site?

| Strong Signals | Weak Signals |
|---|---|
| Clear contact information, physical address | No contact page or hidden ownership |
| Privacy policy, terms of service, transparent ownership | No legal pages or cookie notice |
| Publication and last-updated dates visible | No date stamps or old content presented as current |
| Transparent corrections (change notes) | Content silently corrected without acknowledgment |
| Health/legal disclaimers where appropriate | Promises specific outcomes ("guaranteed results") |
| Clear distinction between fact and opinion | Presents opinion as fact; conflates anecdote with evidence |
| HTTPS, secure site | No HTTPS or mixed content warnings |

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Is it clear who runs the site and how to contact them?
- [ ] Are dates present and recent enough for the topic?
- [ ] Is fact vs. opinion clearly separated?
- [ ] Are appropriate disclaimers present (medical, affiliate, etc.)?

## Google's "Who / How / Why" Test

Before scoring factors individually, ask these three questions:

| Question | Pass Condition |
|---|---|
| **Who** created it? | Visible byline, credentials. Non-negotiable for YMYL. |
| **How** was it created? | Process disclosure (especially important for AI-assisted content). Original research or lived experience. |
| **Why** does it exist? | "To help people" rather than "to attract search clicks." |

> These come from Google's Helpful Content guidelines (merged into core ranking March 2024).

## Content Quality Scoring (XX/100)

Score each factor 0-25, then sum:

| Factor | Score Range | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Experience | 0-25 | First-hand signals |
| Expertise | 0-25 | Credentials + depth |
| Authoritativeness | 0-25 | External recognition |
| Trustworthiness | 0-25 | Transparency + safety |
| **Total** | **0-100** | |

### Scoring Rubric

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 21-25 | Excellent — multiple strong signals, no gaps |
| 16-20 | Good — adequate signals, minor gaps |
| 11-15 | Needs improvement — some signals present but significant gaps |
| 6-10 | Poor — few signals, major gaps |
| 0-5 | Very poor — no detectable signals |

**Minimum acceptable: 15/25 per factor.** If any factor scores below 15, revise before publishing.

## AI Content Assessment Markers

Google's raters (as of Sept 2025 QRG) assess whether content appears AI-generated:

### Low-Quality AI Content Markers
- Generic phrasing, lack of specificity
- No original insight or unique perspective
- Repetitive structure across pages
- No author attribution or generic team byline
- Factual inaccuracies or hallucinated references
- Overly verbose without adding substance

### Acceptable AI-Assisted Content
- Demonstrates genuine E-E-A-T via human oversight
- Provides unique value beyond what AI alone generates
- Has named human author with editorial review
- Contains original insights, data, or experience

## Readability & Structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Flesch Reading Ease | 60-70 (plain English) |
| Sentence length | 15-20 words average |
| Paragraph length | 2-4 sentences |
| Heading hierarchy | H1 → H2 → H3, never skip levels |

---

## Keyword Optimization

- Primary keyword in H1, first 100 words, and at least one H2
- Semantic variations naturally throughout
- 1-3% density — natural, not forced
- No keyword stuffing

---

## Internal & External Linking

| Type | Standard |
|---|---|
| Internal links | 3-5 per 1000 words, descriptive anchor text |
| External links | Cite authoritative sources, open in new tab |
| Orphan pages | None — every page should have incoming and outgoing links |

---

## AI Citation Readiness (GEO)

For content visibility in Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity, and AI Mode:

| Signal | Standard |
|---|---|
| Answer block length | 134-167 words per self-contained answer |
| Direct answer position | Core answer within first 40-60 words of body |
| Question-based H2s | "What is X?", "How does X work?", "Is X safe?" |
| Definition density | Explicit "X is..." statements early in content |
| Specific stats | Use real numbers, not approximations |
| Tables & lists | Markdown tables for comparison data |
| Multi-modal | Pair text with relevant images |

---

## Content Freshness

- Publication date visible
- Last updated date if content was revised
- Content >12 months without update → stale flag (for fast-changing topics)
- For GEO/AI: content <3 months old is 3x more likely to be cited by AI
